\begin{frame}{What Is Metabolic Engineering?}
  \hilite{Metabolic engineering} is the directed modification of an
  organism's metabolic pathways through genetic and regulatory changes
  to increase production of existing products or enable the production of new products.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  The engineering question is: how can we redirect carbon, nitrogen, and
  energy toward a target molecule while meeting the cell's other requirements?

  \vspace{0.8em}
  A \hilite{metabolic flux} is a reaction rate expressed on a specified
  volume or biomass basis. Flux balance analysis estimates these rates
  subject to material balances and flux bounds, for a chosen objective.

  \slidenote{Early work:}{\href{https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2047876/}{Bailey (1991)};
    \href{https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1904627/}{Stephanopoulos and Vallino (1991)}.}
  \lecturesource{Metabolic Engineering: definition, engineering question, and fluxes.
    Bailey, Science 252, 1668–1675 (1991).
    Stephanopoulos and Vallino, Science 252, 1675–1681 (1991).}
\end{frame}

% Flux Balance Analysis and its network/data subsections.
\begin{frame}{Flux Balance Analysis}
  A metabolic reconstruction lists the reactions an organism can carry out.
  The list alone does not specify their rates.

  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.8em}
    \item We express metabolite balances as \hilite{algebraic constraints}.
    \item We restrict reaction rates using \hilite{flux bounds}.
    \item We choose an \hilite{objective}, such as maximizing product export,
      to select an optimal flux distribution.
  \end{enumerate}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Several flux distributions may satisfy the same constraints. The objective
  supplies the criterion for comparing them.
  \lecturesource{Flux Balance Analysis: opening discussion.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stoichiometric Matrix}
  Let $\mathcal M$ be the set of modeled species and $\mathcal R$ the set of
  reactions. The entry $\sigma_{ij}$ is the net coefficient of species $i$
  in reaction $j$. We collect these coefficients in the matrix:
  \[
    \mathbf S=[\sigma_{ij}]
      \in\mathbb R^{|\mathcal M|\times|\mathcal R|}.
  \]

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.65em}
    \item One \hilite{column} describes a reaction. One \hilite{row}
      collects contributions to a species balance.
    \item A positive $\sigma_{ij}$ means reaction $j$ produces species $i$;
      a negative value means the reaction consumes it.
    \item A zero $\sigma_{ij}$ means there is no net change in species $i$,
      including when its reactant and product coefficients cancel.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.6em}
  The coefficient signs refer to the written forward direction of each reaction.
  The matrix records net stoichiometry, not every catalytic or regulatory interaction.
  \lecturesource{Stoichiometric matrix: definition and coefficient signs.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{One Reaction Gives One Matrix Column}
  Reaction $j$ consumes one unit of $A$ and two units of $B$ to produce one unit of $C$.
  With rows ordered as $(A,B,C)$, the reaction column $\mathbf s_j$ is given by:
  \[
    A+2B\longrightarrow C,
    \qquad
    \mathbf s_j=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\-2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
  \]
  Let $\hat v_j$ be the reaction flux. Its contribution to the balances is:
  \[
    \mathbf s_j\hat v_j
      =\begin{bmatrix}-\hat v_j\\-2\hat v_j\\\hat v_j\end{bmatrix}.
  \]
  At positive flux, $B$ is consumed at twice the reaction rate.
  For a reversible reaction, a negative flux reverses the production and
  consumption rates. The stoichiometric column stays the same.
  \lecturesource{Stoichiometric matrix: A + 2B to C example and reverse-flux interpretation.}
\end{frame}

% Flux Balance Analysis Formulation: assumptions, LP, and objective convention.
\begin{frame}{The Flux Balance Approximation}
  We assume that intracellular metabolite amounts per unit biomass remain
  constant. We neglect explicit growth dilution and include transport and
  exchange reactions in the network.

  \vspace{0.5em}
  The vector $\hat{\mathbf v}$ collects the reaction fluxes $\hat v_j$.
  For each balanced species $i\in\mathcal M$, production and consumption
  cancel across reactions $j\in\mathcal R$:
  \[
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal R}\sigma_{ij}\hat v_j=0,
    \qquad\text{or}\qquad
    \mathbf S\hat{\mathbf v}=\mathbf 0.
  \]

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Transport reactions contribute to the same balances as the other reactions.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  Metabolite amounts per unit biomass remain constant even while reactions continue.
  \slidenote{Derivation:}{\href{\derivationlink}{Balance assumptions and the conventional FBA approximation}}
  \lecturesource{Flux Balance Analysis Formulation: assumptions.
    \href{\derivationlink}{Companion balance derivation.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Flux Balance Analysis Formulation}
  Let $c_j$ be the objective weight of reaction $j$, and let
  $\mathcal L_j$ and $\mathcal U_j$ be its prescribed flux bounds.
  With the objective weights and flux bounds fixed, the linear program is:
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \underset{\hat{\mathbf v}}{\operatorname{maximize}}\quad
        &\sum_{j\in\mathcal R}c_j\hat v_j\\[3pt]
      \text{subject to}\quad
        &\sum_{j\in\mathcal R}\sigma_{ij}\hat v_j=0,
          &&i\in\mathcal M,\\[3pt]
        &\mathcal L_j\leq\hat v_j\leq\mathcal U_j,
          &&j\in\mathcal R.
    \end{aligned}
  \]
  A \hilite{feasible} distribution satisfies all balances and bounds.
  An \hilite{optimal} distribution attains the best objective value
  and need not be unique.
  \slidenote{Readings:}{\href{https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3108565/}{Orth et al. (2010)};
    \href{https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30787451/}{Heirendt et al. (2019)}.}
  \lecturesource{Flux Balance Analysis Formulation: linear program and interpretation.
    Orth et al. (2010), What is flux balance analysis?
    Heirendt et al. (2019), COBRA Toolbox v3.0.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An Objective for Product Export}
  Let $p$ denote the urea exchange reaction, \texttt{b4}. We write this reaction
  as uptake from the surroundings, so \hilite{negative flux represents export}.
  We set its objective weight to $-1$ and all other weights to zero:
  \[
    c_j=\begin{cases}-1,&j=p,\\0,&j\neq p,\end{cases}
    \qquad
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal R}c_j\hat v_j=-\hat v_p.
  \]

  \vspace{0.5em}
  Maximizing $-\hat v_p$ makes the urea exchange flux as negative as the
  balances and bounds allow, which maximizes urea export.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  We express metabolic fluxes in $\mathrm{mmol\,gDW^{-1}\,h^{-1}}$,
  where gDW means grams of dry biomass. Each bound has the units of its flux.
  \lecturesource{Flux Balance Analysis Formulation: uptake-positive exchange convention,
    urea-export objective, and flux units.}
\end{frame}

% A model for flux bounds: retain the instructor's brace notation.
\begin{frame}{A Model for Flux Bounds}
  For enzyme-catalyzed reaction $j$, we combine the reference capacity
  $V_{max,j}^{\circ}$, relative enzyme abundance $e/e^{\circ}$, activity factor
  $\theta_j$, saturation factor $f_j$, and reversibility parameter $\delta_j$
  to define the flux bounds:
  \[
    \underbrace{
      -\delta_j\overbrace{
        \left[V_{max,j}^{\circ}\left(\frac{e}{e^{\circ}}\right)
        \theta_j(\dots)f_j(\dots)\right]
      }^{\text{reverse: other functions or parameters?}}
    }_{\mathcal L_j}
    \leq\hat v_j\leq
    \underbrace{
      V_{max,j}^{\circ}\left(\frac{e}{e^{\circ}}\right)
      \theta_j(\dots)f_j(\dots)
    }_{\mathcal U_j}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.8em}
  This model assumes equal forward and reverse capacities for a reversible
  reaction. If the maximum rates differ, we need separate capacity estimates
  for the two directions.

  \vspace{0.6em}
  We specify the parameters, enzyme abundances, and activity and saturation
  factors \hilite{before solving} the linear program.
  \lecturesource{A model for flux bounds: annotated bound model and fixed-parameter assumption.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reference Enzyme Capacity}
  Let $k_{cat,j}^{\circ}$ be the reference catalytic turnover number and
  $e^{\circ}$ the characteristic enzyme abundance. The reference capacity is:
  \[
    V_{max,j}^{\circ}=k_{cat,j}^{\circ}e^{\circ}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.4em}
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lYl@{}}
    \toprule
    Quantity & Meaning & Units\\
    \midrule
    $V_{max,j}^{\circ}$ & This is the maximum rate at reference enzyme abundance. & $\mathrm{mmol\,gDW^{-1}\,h^{-1}}$\\
    $k_{cat,j}^{\circ}$ & This is the turnover rate at substrate saturation. & $\mathrm{h^{-1}}$\\
    $e^{\circ},\ e$ & $e^{\circ}$ and $e$ denote reference and actual abundance. & $\mathrm{mmol\,gDW^{-1}}$\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}

  \vspace{0.4em}
  The ratio $e/e^{\circ}$ scales the reference capacity to the actual
  abundance of the enzyme catalyzing reaction $j$.
  \lecturesource{A model for flux bounds: maximum-velocity equation and nomenclature table.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Activity, Saturation, and Reversibility}
  The activity factor $\theta_j$, saturation factor $f_j$, and reversibility parameter $\delta_j$ are dimensionless:

  \vspace{0.6em}
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lY@{}}
    \toprule
    Quantity & Meaning\\
    \midrule
    $\theta_j(\dots)\in[0,1]$ & The activity factor gives the fraction of maximal activity available under allosteric regulation.\\
    $f_j(\dots)\in[0,1]$ & The saturation factor describes substrate availability. A value of one represents saturating substrates.\\
    $\delta_j\in\{0,1\}$ & The reversibility parameter is zero for a forward-only reaction and one when reverse flux is allowed.\\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}

  \vspace{0.8em}
  Activity and substrate availability can reduce the capacity below
  $V_{max,j}^{\circ}(e/e^{\circ})$.
  \lecturesource{A model for flux bounds: nomenclature for theta, f, and delta.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Simplified Bounds Model}
  We assume the reference enzyme abundance, no allosteric effects, and
  saturating substrates. Setting $e/e^{\circ}=\theta_j=f_j=1$ gives the bounds:
  \[
    \underbrace{-\delta_j V_{max,j}^{\circ}}_{\mathcal L_j}
      \leq\hat v_j\leq
    \underbrace{V_{max,j}^{\circ}}_{\mathcal U_j}.
  \]

  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.8em}
    \item For a \hilite{forward-only reaction}, $\delta_j=0$, so the flux satisfies
      $0\leq\hat v_j\leq V_{max,j}^{\circ}$.
    \item For a \hilite{reversible reaction}, $\delta_j=1$, so the flux satisfies
      $-V_{max,j}^{\circ}\leq\hat v_j\leq V_{max,j}^{\circ}$.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  We need estimates of the turnover number, characteristic enzyme abundance,
  and reaction reversibility.
  \lecturesource{Simplified bounds model: assumptions and bound equation.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exchange Bounds Specify the Surroundings}
  Exchange bounds limit nutrient uptake and product secretion. In the urea
  example, we write every exchange as uptake from the surroundings,
  so \hilite{positive flux represents uptake} and negative flux represents export.

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.8em}
    \item The upper bound limits the uptake rate.
    \item An upper bound of zero prevents uptake through that reaction.
    \item A negative lower bound sets the largest allowed export rate.
    \item A lower bound of zero prevents export through that reaction.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  The exchange bounds specify which nutrients the system can take up and
  which products it can release. Internal reactions have separate bounds
  based on enzyme capacity and reaction direction.
  \lecturesource{Simplified bounds model: exchange bounds, using the uptake-positive
    convention in the urea-cycle example.}
\end{frame}

% Summary: opening, exactly three takeaways, and the general modeling lesson.
\begin{frame}{Summary}
  We formulated flux balance analysis as a linear program, combining a
  metabolic network, biological constraints, and a production objective.

  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\large\textbf{\ink{Key takeaways}}}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
    \item \textbf{\ink{Stoichiometric matrix.}} We organized net production and
      consumption coefficients into a matrix to write the material balances.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Flux balance analysis.}} We combined material balances,
      flux bounds, and a linear objective to estimate an optimal flux
      distribution, which need not be unique.
    \item \textbf{\ink{Flux bounds.}} We used enzyme capacity and reaction
      direction to limit the allowed rates. We simplified the bounds by assuming
      reference enzyme abundance, no allosteric effects, and saturating substrates.
  \end{itemize}

  \vspace{0.3em}
  The same reaction network can support different production rates under
  different constraints. An optimal solution describes what the model allows
  under the conditions we specify.
  \lecturesource{Summary: opening, three key takeaways, and closing general lesson.}
\end{frame}
